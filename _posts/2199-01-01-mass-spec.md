The notion of *mass* in physics that we are familiar with nowadays can be traced back to what was called *quantity of matter* defined by Newton in his masterpiece *[Principia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophiæ_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica)* related to a measure of an body's resistance to the net force exertion. With the development of modern physics, our understanding of this intrinsic property, along with Newton's conceptions of space and time, has been substantially revised and extended, and it has gradually led to the concepts of Lorentz-invariant quantities and parameters characterizing wave propagation. In this blog, I collect and explore several aspects of the mass spectrum in quantum field theory. The first few parts develop the subject from a general perspective, while the final part examines a concrete example from integrable field theory.

# Elementary Set-up

To begin with, let us briefly introduce some necessary concepts related to our discussion. In quantum field theory, **mass** is associated with the Casimir operator of the Poincaré group


$$
\begin{equation}
M^2:=P^{\mu}P_{\mu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}P^{\mu} P^{\nu}
\label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}
$$


where $$P^{\mu}$$ generates the space-time translation and $$\eta_{\mu\nu}$$ is the Minkowskian metric. The generators $$P^{\mu}$$ form an Abelian subalgebra of the whole Minkowskian spacetime symmetric algebra, $$i.e.$$ $$[P^{\mu},P^{\nu}]=0$$ for any pair of Lorentz indices, thus all these operators can be diagonalized simultaneously. More specifically, given a state $$\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}$$ where $$\mathcal{H}$$ is the Hilbert space of the theory, we have
$$
\begin{equation}
P^{\mu}\ket{\psi}=p^{\mu}\ket{\psi},\qquad M^2\ket{\psi}=m^2\ket{\psi}
\end{equation}
$$
which then decomposes the Hilbert space into some subspaces $$\mathcal{H}_{p}$$ via a Lorentz invariant measure $$\mu$$, or equivalently $$\mathcal{H}_{m^2}$$ via a measure $$\nu$$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}\cong\int^{\oplus}_{V}\mathrm{d}\mu(p)\mathcal{H}_{p}=\int^{\oplus}_{\sigma(M^2)}\mathrm{d}\nu(m^2)\mathcal{H}_{m^2}
\end{equation}
$$
In the expression above, $$V$$ denotes all orbits of $$p$$ under the Lorentz transformation $$p\mapsto \Lambda\cdot p$$ and $$\sigma(M^2)$$ is the spectrum of the linear operator on the Hilbert space $$M^2:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}$$.  One should notice here that $$V$$ and $$\sigma(M^2)$$ may contain some unphysical orbits or values since no physical constraints have been imposed. To be realized as a physical theory, we thus constrain the Hilbert space through the spectrum of $$P^{\mu}$$:
$$
\begin{equation}
\sigma(P^{\mu})\subset\{p^{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{1,D-1} |p^0\geq 0 \text{ and } p^{\mu}p_{\mu}\geq 0\}
\end{equation}
$$
which immediately means $$\sigma(M^2)\subset[0,\infty)$$. Furthermore, to equip the theorem a realisation of particles, there are some other conditions naturally required. The most directly, if the model contains some massive particle states, there should be a lower bound for the spectral corresponding to the mass $$m$$ of a single-particle state. A state formed by 2 or more particles result in a continuous spectral higher than or equal to $$4m^2$$, possiblely together with a list of discrete values associated to a set of **bound states** $$B$$. In conclusion, the spectrum is given by
$$
\begin{equation}
\sigma(M^2) = \{m^2,(m_{b}^2)_{b\in B}\} \cup[4m^2,\infty), \qquad \text{where }m_{b}\in(m^2,4m^2)
\end{equation}
$$

In this note, we will be interested in the analytical structure of the physical observables related to the mass spectrum, and to this end, let us start by introducing two related theorems.

**Theorem** (Källen, Lehmann): Let $$\ket{\Omega}$$ be the vacuum and $$\mathcal{O}(x)$$ be a local operator transforming under the adjoint action of the Poincaré group. Then two point function of  $$\mathcal{O}(x)$$ has the following structure:
$$
\begin{equation}
iG_+^{(\mathcal{O})}(x):=\bra{\Omega}\mathcal{O}(x)\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}(0)\ket{\Omega}=|v_{\mathcal{O}}|^2+\int_0^\infty \mathrm{d} \rho(m^2)iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_+(x;m^2)
\end{equation}
$$
Here, $$v_{\mathcal{O}}=\bra{\Omega}\mathcal{O}(0)\ket{\Omega}$$ is the vacuum expectation value, which is usually normalized according to $$v_{\phi}$$. The positive measure $$\mathrm{d}\rho$$ satisfies
$$
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{supp}(\mathrm{d}\rho)\subset\sigma(M^2) \subset [0,\infty)
\end{equation}
$$
and one can deduce that
$$
\begin{equation}
iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_+(x;m^2)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D-1}}\int \frac{\mathrm{d}^{D-1}\mathbf{p}}{2\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2+m^2}}e^{-ipx}
\end{equation}
$$

> *Proof*: Let $$\mathrm{d}\eta$$ be the spectral measure for $$P^{\mu}$$, which means $$P^{\mu}=\int_V\mathrm{d}\eta(p)p^{\mu}$$. Then, because of the Lorentz invariance of the  vacuum, we have
> $$
> \begin{equation}
> iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_+(x)=\bra{\Omega}\mathcal{O}(0)e^{-iP^{\mu}x_{\mu}}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}(0)\ket{\Omega}=\int_Ve^{-ip^{\mu}x_{\mu}}\bra{\Omega}\mathcal{O}(0)\mathrm{d}\eta(p)\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}(0)\ket{\Omega}
> \end{equation}
> $$
> Let us define $$\mathrm{d}\nu(p):=\bra{\Omega}\mathcal{O}(0)\mathrm{d}\eta(p)\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}(0)\ket{\Omega}$$ which is immediately Lorentz invariant and positive. There is, in fact, a unique measure up to scalings:
> $$
> \begin{equation}
> \mathrm{d}\mu(p) =\delta^{(D)}(p)\mathrm{d}^Dp
> \end{equation}
> $$
> or on the orbits of $$m^2\geq0$$ (a measure in the same form for space-like orbits with $$m^2<0$$ is apparently not Lorentz invariant):
> $$
> \begin{equation}
> \mathrm{d}\mu(p;m^2):=\theta(p^0)\delta(p^2-m^2)\mathrm{d}^Dp=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D-1}\mathbf{p}}{2\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2+m^2}}
> \end{equation}
> $$
> Therefore, $$\mathrm{d}\nu(p)$$ is a linear combination of the above two measures, thus let us denote
> $$
> \mathrm{d}\nu(p)=\rho_0\delta^{(D)}(p)\mathrm{d}^Dp+\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D-1}}\int_0^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\rho(m^2)\frac{\mathrm{d}^{D-1}\mathbf{p}}{2\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2+m^2}}
> $$
> In fact, $$\rho_0=\nu(\{0\})=\bra{\Omega}\mathcal{O}(0)\eta\{0\}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}(0)\ket{\Omega}$$. Because of the uniqueness of the vacuum, $$\eta(\{0\})=\ket{\Omega}\bra{\Omega}$$, then we have $$\rho_0=|\bra{\Omega}\mathcal{O}(0)\ket{\Omega}|^2$$. 

**Theorem** (Lehmann, Symanzik and Zimmermann): There exist two asymptotatic Hilbert spaces $$\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}}$$ and $$\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}}$$, on each of which the mass spectrum satisfies
$$
\begin{equation}
\sigma(M^2) = \{m^2,(m_{b}^2)_{b\in B}\} \cup[4m^2,\infty)
\end{equation}
$$

> *Remark*: In fact, the structure of mass spectrums in the asymptotic regions is a very short conclusion as a consequence of the full Lehmann-Symanzik-Zimmermann (LSZ) conditions, which we choose not to present here. The idea follows from that a state of particles satisfies
> $$
> \begin{equation}
> P^{\mu}\ket{p_1,\ldots,p_n}_{\text{as}} = (\sum_{i=1}^np_i^{\mu})\ket{p_1,\ldots,p_n}_{\text{as}},\qquad \text{as}=\text{in/out}
> \end{equation}
> $$
> where $$m$$ is the value for $$n=1$$ and the others correspond to masses of multi particles. 

In some theories, there are different types of particles where particles with $$m^2_b>m^2$$ are not necessarily truly bond states. In such cases, we may interpret $$m^2$$ as the lowest mass value, while the spectrum structure still holds.

Based on the aforementioned two theorems, we deduce that the two point function should be equipped with the following structure:
$$
\begin{equation}
iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_+(x)=|v_{\mathcal{O}}|^2+iZG^{(\mathcal{O})}_+(x;m^2)+i\sum_{b\in B}Z_bG^{(\mathcal{O})}_{+}(x;m_b^2)+\int_{4m^2}^{\infty} \mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(m'^2)iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_{+}(x;m'^2)
\end{equation}
$$
We can also consider the time-ordered two-point correlation functions defined by
$$
\begin{equation}
iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_{F}(x)=\bra{\Omega}T\{\mathcal{O}(x)\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}(0)\}\ket{\Omega}=\theta(x^0)iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_+(x)+\theta(-x^0)iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_+(-x)
\end{equation}
$$
whose expression can be well represented in terms of some Feynman prescriptions: 
$$
\begin{equation}
\int \frac{\mathrm{d}^{D-1}\mathbf{p}}{2\sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2+m^2}}(e^{-ipx}\theta(x^0)+e^{+ipx}\theta(-x^0)) = \frac{i}{2\pi}\int \frac{\mathrm{d}^D p}{p^2-m^2+i0^+}e^{-ipx}
\end{equation}
$$
One can observe that the mass spectrum is closely related to the analytical structure of $$iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_F(x)$$:
$$
\begin{equation}
iG^{(\mathcal{O})}_F (x)=|v_{\mathcal{O}}|^2+\sum_{\mu\in\{m,(m_{b})_{b\in B}\}}Z_{\mu}\int \frac{\mathrm{d}^Dp}{(2\pi)^D}\frac{ie^{-ipx}}{p^2-\mu^2+i0^+}+\int_{4m^2}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(m'^2)\int \frac{\mathrm{d}^Dp}{(2\pi)^D}\frac{ie^{-ipx}}{p^2-m'^2+i0^+}
\end{equation}
$$
In the momentum space, up to the vacuum expectation value, $$iG_F^{(\mathcal{O})}(x)-|v_{\mathcal{O}}|^2$$ is then transformed into
$$
\begin{equation}
i\tilde{G}^{(\mathcal{O})}_F(p^2) =\sum_{\mu\in\{m,(m_{b})_{b\in B}\}}Z_{\mu}\frac{i}{p^2-\mu^2+i0^+}+\int_{4m^2}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(m'^2)\frac{i}{p^2-m'^2+i0^+}
\end{equation}
$$
It shows that, if we consider $$p^2\in\mathbb{C}$$, the discrete eigenvalues of $$M^2$$ is related to some poles of the Fourier transformed $$i\tilde{G}^{(\mathcal{O})}_F(p^2)$$, and that there exists a branch cut $$[4m^2,\infty)$$. In other words, we obtain an analytical function on $$\mathbb{C}\backslash\sigma(M^2)$$. Notice that under this setup the Lorentz invariant $$p^2$$ is generally *off-shell*, and the on-shell condition $$p^2=m^2\in\sigma(M^2)$$ is however indicated by the branch structure of the function above. 

Conversely, one might be also interested in the class of functions $$G(p^2)$$ equipped with such analytical structures. For example, mathematically, one may ask that to what extent we can determine a complex function by virtues of its analytical domain and branch points/cuts. This idea guids us to exploit the analytical properties generated by the fundamental physical quantities to understand the observables in the targeted quantum field theory. We hope that this idea can play a role in extracting, computing and constructing those more complicated physical observables, such as multi-point correlation functions and form factors, which are generally very difficult to deal with especially in cases of interacting field theory. Brute force computation doesn't always guarantee closed-form results, even nor the existence of well-defined expressions. However, there have been plenty of evidence that such barriers do not expel the existence of results, and it seems that the difficulty sometimes comes from the lack of knowledge or usage of the underlying structures, which are either hidden or explicit, equipped in the theory of interest. The story in quantum chromodynamics about the [Parke-Taylor amplitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MHV_amplitudes) is actually in my favor as a specific example. 

# Singularities of the $$S$$-matrix

In the previous section, we briefly demonstrated the properties that a two-point correlation function should follow once a quantum field theory is given and the spacetime symmetries are respected. A natural question is then, if we instead have a list of properties that the physical observables obey, that can we construct a theory that respect all the fundamental principles and symmetries of quantum field theory? This question is particularly interesting in a certain class of quantum field theories associated with a large group of symmetries, which lead to **integrable** field theories. One of the most characteristic properties of such class of models is the *facoterized scattering*, which states that all the scattering processes are fully determined by a 2-body $$S$$-matrix. 

Recall that there are two asymptotic regions with $$\mathcal{H}_{\text{in}}$$ and $$\mathcal{H}_{\text{out}}$$, and each of them ought to be complete to carry all the possible physical degrees of freedom of the theory under the consideration. Here we should be clear that these Hilbert spaces that we mentioned are in principle some **Fork spaces** which can be decomposed into subspaces with well-defined particle numbers. Depending on the physical models, there might be also some other degrees of freedom corresponding to different types of particles in which cases the mass $m$ is not sufficient to characterize the representation.  

Consider a two-body scattering $$A(p_1)+B(p_2)\to A(q_1)+B(q_2)$$ described by the overlap $$_\text{out}\bra{q_1,q_2}\ket{p_1,p_2}_{\text{in}}$$. The $$S$$-matrix links the two asymptotic spaces by
$$
\begin{equation}
S\ket{p_1,p_2}_{\text{out}}=\ket{p_1,p_2}_{\text{in}},\qquad p_1+p_2=q_1+q_2
\end{equation}
$$
The $$S$$-matrix satisfies immediately the unitarity and the crossing symmetries which we do not discuss here. Here, we are interested in the singularities and zeros of the two-particle $S$-matrix. In fact, due to the Lorentz invariance, the $S$-matrix should be a function of Mandelstam variables $$s=(p_1+p_2)^2$$, $$t=(p_1-q_1)^2$$ and $$u=(p_1-q_2)^2$$. In fact, there are only two independent variables since $$s+t+u=2(m_A^2+m_B^2)$$, so it is sufficient to write it as a function as $$s$$ and $$t$$. To investigate the analytical properties of this $$S$$-matrix, we introduce a theorem that links the matrix elements and the $$i\tilde{G}^{(\mathcal{O})}_{F}(p^2)$$ we introduced before.

**Theorem** (LSZ reduction) Let $$p_1^2=q_1^2=m_1^2$$ and $$p_2^2=q_2^2=m_2^2$$. The $$S$$-matrix elements can be obtained by imposing on-shell conditions on the time-ordered four-point function of asymptotical particles. Namely, define
$$
\begin{equation}
iG_F(y_1,y_2;x_1,x_2):=\bra{\Omega}T\{\phi(y_1)\phi(y_2)\phi(x_1)\phi(x_2)\}\ket{\Omega}
\end{equation}
$$
and its Fourier transform
$$
\begin{equation}
i\tilde{G}(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)=\int\prod_{i=1}^2\mathrm{d}^Dx_i\mathrm{d}^Dy_ie^{-i\sum_{i=1}^2(p_i\cdot x_i+q_i\cdot y_i)}iG_{F}(y_1,y_2;x_1,x_2)
\end{equation}
$$
Then one has
$$
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
_{\text{out}\!}\bra{q_1,q_2}\ket{p_1,p_2}_{\text{in}}&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2(D-1)}Z_AZ_B}\int\prod_{i=1}^2\mathrm{d}^{D}y_i\mathrm{d}^{D}x_i(\partial_{y_i}^2+m^2)(\partial_{x_i}^2+m^2)iG_F(y_1,y_2;x_1,x_2)
\\
&=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{2}(p_i^2-m_i^2)(q_i^2-m_i^2)}{(2\pi)^{2(D-1)}Z_AZ_B}\times i\tilde{G}(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
$$

# Sine-Gordon Field Theory

**Bound states** In the 1+1 dimensional space time, it is useful to parameterize the relativistic momentum of particles through *rapidities* $$\theta\in\mathbb{R}$$, $$i.e.$$
$$
\begin{equation}
p = m(\cosh \theta, \sinh \theta)
\end{equation}
$$
Given two particles with $$p_1$$ and $$p_2$$, the summation of their momenta is then
$$
\begin{equation}
p_1+p_2 = (m_1\cosh\theta_1+m_2\cosh\theta_2,m_1\sinh\theta_1+m_2\sinh\theta_2)
\end{equation}
$$
which then forms an invariant mass as
$$
\begin{equation}
M^2=(p_1+p_2)^2=m_1^2+m_2^2+2m_1m_2\cosh\theta_{12},\qquad\theta_{12}:=\theta_1-\theta_2
\end{equation}
$$
Clearly, in any general case with real rapidities,  $$M^2\geq (m_1+m_2)^2$$. Therefore, the existence of the bound states should be related to some complex rapidities. In addition, to ensure $M^2$ is real, we immediately know that $$\theta_{12}\in i\mathbb{R}$$. We will denote the bound state by $$\theta_{12}=i\theta_{(12)}$$ for a $$\theta_{(12)}\in [0,\pi]$$ corresponding to $$(m_1-m_2)^2\leq M_{(12)}^2\leq (m_1+m_2)^2$$. In some context, one may call the imaginary number $$\theta_{(12)}$$ a *fusion angle*. 

Let $$\theta_1=\alpha+i\gamma$$ and $$\theta_2=\alpha+i\delta$$ where $$\alpha\in\mathbb{R}$$ and $$\gamma,\delta\in [-\pi/2,\pi/2]$$. The reality of $$p_1+p_2$$ provides a further constraint:
$$
\begin{equation}
m_1\sin\gamma+m_2\sin\delta=0,\qquad \text{and } \gamma-\delta=\theta_{(12)}
\end{equation}
$$
which is solved by
$$
\begin{equation}
\tan\gamma =\frac{m_2\sin\theta_{(12)}}{m_1+m_2\cos\theta_{(12)}} ,\qquad \tan \delta = -\frac{m_1\sin\theta_{(12)}}{m_1\cos\theta_{(12)}+m_2}
\end{equation}
$$
In particular, if this bound state is formed by two particles of the same type, $$i.e.$$ $$m_1=m_2=m$$ for (anti-)solitons of the sine-Gordon field theory, one can immediately observe
$$
\begin{equation}
\gamma=-\delta=\frac{1}{2}\theta_{(12)}
\end{equation}
$$
Consider now $$m_1=m$$ and $$m_2=2m\cos(u_k/2)$$ to describe the scattering between a breather and a soliton or an anti-soliton. The formulae above tells as that
$$
\begin{equation}
M^2_{b_k,\nu}=4m^2(\frac{1}{4}+(\cos\frac{u_k}{2})^2+\frac{1}{2}\cos{\frac{u_k}{2}}\cos\theta_{(b_k\nu)})=4m^2\cos^2\frac{u_n}{2}
\end{equation}
$$
for some bound state $$u_n$$. However, none of the poles of $$S_{b_k,\nu}(\theta)$$ can make the equality hold. 





Consider now two breathers $$m_1= 2m\cos(u_k/2)$$ and $$m_1= 2m\cos(u_l/2)$$ corresponding to $$\theta_{(b)}=u_{b}$$ for $$b=k,l$$, together with a fusion angle $$\theta_{(k+l)}$$. The invariant mass of this two-particle system is then given by
$$
\begin{equation}
M^2_{k+l} = 4m^2\left((\cos\frac{u_k}{2})^2+(\cos\frac{u_l}{2})^2+2\cos\frac{u_k}{2}\cos\frac{u_l}{2}\cos{\theta_{(k+l)}}\right) \equiv 4m^2\cos^2\frac{u_{k+l}}{2}
\end{equation}
$$
where the second equation is the *consistency condition* that we expect, which ensures the formation of a bound state through the process $$b_k+b_l\to b_{k+l}$$. It is direct to solve this equation, which gives
$$
\begin{equation}
\theta_{(k+l)} = \pi-\frac{u_k+u_l}{2} = \frac{k+l}{2}\xi
\end{equation}
$$
as well as (from the equations for the fusion angle)
$$
\begin{equation}
\Im(\theta_{b_k}) = \frac{l\xi}{2},\qquad \Im(\theta_{b_l})=-\frac{k\xi}{2}
\end{equation}
$$
The reason why we don't consider the formation of lower bound states (say $$k+l-2p$$) is that such a fusion process is related to a double pole of the breather-breather $$S$$-matrix.
